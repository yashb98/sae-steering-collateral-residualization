# Related work: who has done this before, what they did better, what to take

Initial assessment written 2026-09-06 from the arXiv abstracts and, where noted, the full HTML of each paper. Every number below was read from the source that day. "Ours" means the four-setting collateral-side residualization in github.com/yashb98/sae-steering-collateral-residualization.


Updated 2026-09-07: [the new source-verified sweep](RECENT_RESEARCH_2026-09-07.md) adds directly relevant work through 4 September. Ong et al. forecast behavioral side effects before steering; Upadhyaya and Sikdar test geometry-linked language collateral and a low-entanglement feature control. These substantially narrow the earlier novelty framing. The completed robustness checks are reported in the experiment repository's RESULTS.md; the ranked additions below record the initial proposal. The dated numerical comparisons below remain the 6 September assessment unless explicitly corrected.

## 1. Closest prior work

**Chalnev, Siu and Conmy, "Improving Steering Vectors by Targeting Sparse Autoencoder Features" (arXiv:2411.02193, Nov 2024; full text read).** The closest methodological precedent. They define the effect of a steering vector as the mean change in SAE feature activations between steered and unsteered rollouts (896 rollouts of 32 tokens, Gemma-2-2B, steering at layer 12, read through a 16k JumpReLU SAE at the same layer). They then fit a linear "effect approximator", a d_model by d_sae map from steering direction to predicted feature effects, trained on 50,000 decoder vectors of a 65k SAE, and use it to build SAE-Targeted Steering vectors that maximise a target feature while subtracting the predicted side effects. Judged by GPT-4o-mini on behaviour times coherence, SAE-TS scores 0.36 against 0.217 for CAA and 0.129 for plain SAE feature steering, winning 7 of 9 tasks. Their Table 1 shows the failure mode we call collateral: steering a "month" feature moves a 45%-density feature three times more than the intended target (4.58 vs 1.38).

What they did differently and better: side effects are measured on generated continuations at all positions, not at the final token of fixed contexts; the predictor is a learned map of the whole downstream effect vector rather than univariate correlations with a scalar count; the prediction is used to construct cleaner interventions and the result is validated at the output level with a coherence judge. What they did not do: ask which cheap pre-intervention statistics of a feature predict how messy it will be, or control for frequency and effect magnitude. That gap is exactly Duan's paper and our extension.

**Wu et al., AxBench (ICML 2025; arXiv:2501.17148; full text read).** Steering evaluated on 500 concepts with an LLM judge on concept, instruction-following and fluency (each 0 to 2, harmonic mean), a 14-value steering-factor sweep, Gemma-2-2B and 9B. SAE steering averages 0.165 against 0.894 for prompting; "increasing the factor monotonically reduces instruct score in all methods". Better than ours: output-level utility and fluency at scale, and an explicit factor sweep. Missing from ours: any factor sweep (alpha is fixed at 1.0, the paper's Section 3.3 value) and any output-level side-effect metric beyond KL.

**Tan et al., "Analyzing the Generalization and Reliability of Steering Vectors" (arXiv:2407.12404; full text read).** Steerability is a slope over a multiplier sweep, measured per input; distributions are often bimodal with 40 to 50% anti-steerable inputs on many datasets, and spurious token and position biases explain part of the variance. Better than ours: reliability treated as a distribution over inputs with diagnosed spurious drivers. Ours summarises stability as a mean cosine.

**Wang, Hu, Wang and Zou, "Does higher interpretability imply better utility?" (arXiv:2510.03659).** 90 SAEs over Gemma-2-2B, Qwen-2.5-3B and Gemma-2-9B; interpretability (SAEBench) and steering utility (AxBench) rank-agree only weakly (Kendall tau_b about 0.298). Their "Delta Token Confidence", how much amplifying a feature changes the next-token distribution, selects features that steer about 52.5% better. Relevance: this is the direct-logit and KL family of predictors, and it is the closest published statement that a cheap per-feature statistic predicts steering utility.

**Cui, Shen and Yang, "SAE Interventions are Unreliable: Post-Intervention Recovery of Suppressed Behavior" (arXiv:2606.18322, June 2026).** Suppressed behaviour can be recovered through the SAE reconstruction residual (95.8% recovery on refusal steering with defended-feature drift of 0.131). Relevance: our collateral count only sees downstream changes that land on SAE features; changes in the reconstruction residual are invisible to it.

**Mayne, Yang and Mahdi, "Can sparse autoencoders be used to decompose and interpret steering vectors?" (arXiv:2411.08790).** SAE decompositions of steering vectors mislead because steering vectors are out of distribution in norm and can have meaningful negative projections. Relevance: this motivates checking intervention scale. The paper studies SAE decomposition of steering vectors and does not establish that our alpha = 1.0 intervention is out of distribution.

**O'Brien et al., "Steering Language Model Refusal with Sparse Autoencoders" (arXiv:2411.11296).** Refusal steering degrades benchmark performance on safe inputs; refusal features are entangled with capabilities. Relevance: the practical cost of collateral is capability loss, which we never measure.

## 2. Work on decoder geometry as a defect to remove

Chanin et al., "A is for Absorption" (arXiv:2409.14507) define feature splitting and absorption and show that varying size or sparsity does not fix them. OrtSAE (Korznikov et al., arXiv:2509.22033) penalises pairwise decoder cosine similarity during training: absorption down 65%, composition down 15%, mean cosine 2.7 times lower than BatchTopK, 9% more distinct features, spurious-correlation removal up 6%. MetaSAEs (Levinson, arXiv:2604.03436, 2026) penalise decoder directions that a meta-dictionary can reconstruct (7.5% lower mean |phi|, 7.6% better fuzzing on GPT-2 Large). C2R (Jin et al., ICML 2026, arXiv:2606.30609) penalises co-activation of directionally similar latents. Jedryszek and Crook, "Stable and Steerable Sparse Autoencoders with Weight Regularization" (arXiv:2603.04198, 2026) report that L2 weight regularisation roughly doubles steering success on Pythia-70M TopK SAEs while raising cross-seed feature overlap. CircuitSteer (Saadatinia et al., arXiv:2608.05732, 2026) builds multi-layer interventions from co-activation and geometric alignment of decoder directions and is the only method in its comparison that preserves fluency.

Several of these training methods penalize redundant decoder structure; CircuitSteer instead uses aligned features across layers. Duan's paper and ours study geometry as a predictor of steering side effects. The two views motivate a specific causal comparison not located in this sweep: steer the same model through a vanilla dictionary and through an OrtSAE or C2R dictionary and ask whether the lower-crowding dictionary produces less collateral per unit effect. Our KaggleLab post 007 tried a weak, untuned version of this on GPT-2-small and saw no effect, consistent with its own manipulation check failing.

## 3. Work that changes how our labels should be read

**Sun et al., "Dense SAE Latents Are Features, Not Bugs" (NeurIPS 2025; arXiv:2506.15679).** Dense latents track position, bind context, regulate entropy and form antipodal decoder pairs. Frequency is therefore a real confound and dense downstream features are expected collateral sinks, which is what SAE-TS's Table 1 shows. Our control for frequency is justified, but the downstream panel is biased toward frequent features. It is not necessarily dominated by features above a fixed density threshold: the completed exclusion check removes only a small share at the 10% threshold.

**Gerasimov et al., "Unstable Features, Reproducible Subspaces" (arXiv:2606.12138, 2026).** Features that fail to reproduce across SAE seeds are low-frequency surface-form triggers with weak functional impact. Our 300 features come from one seed of each dictionary; a share of them are likely unstable features whose steering behaviour is idiosyncratic to that seed.

**Hoang et al., FEGA (arXiv:2607.24645, 2026).** Removing a feature across contexts and analysing the cloud of logit changes separates value-like features (low-dimensional, structured effects) from pointer-like ones (diffuse effects); "consistent one-dimensional effects are rare". Our signed and absolute stability cosines are one-dimensional summaries of that cloud.

**Korznikov et al., "Sanity Checks for Sparse Autoencoders: Do SAEs Beat Random Baselines?" (arXiv:2602.14111, 2026).** SAEs barely beat random baselines on interpretability (0.90 vs 0.87), sparse probing (0.72 vs 0.69) and causal editing (0.72 vs 0.73), and recover 9% of ground-truth features at 71% explained variance. No random baseline exists in Duan's paper or in ours.

**Chanin, "Are Sparse Autoencoder Benchmarks Reliable?" (arXiv:2605.18229, 2026).** The intervention-based SAEBench metrics TPP and SCR fail reseed-noise, ground-truth and trajectory tests; k-sparse probing is the most dependable. Our labels are intervention-based, and their measurement noise across context draws has never been quantified.

**Duan, "Perplexity Can Miss SAE Feature Damage Under Quantization" (arXiv:2606.03002, 2026).** Evan's other paper: per-feature survival under quantization is predictable from feature statistics with cross-validated AUC 0.92 to 0.97 while perplexity is flat. Same methodology family; worth citing in the revision as the companion result.

## 4. What to take, ranked by value per hour

1. **Label reliability.** Re-measure the labels for the same 300 features on a second independent draw of 48 contexts and report the test-retest Spearman. This estimates repeatability conditional on the chosen sampling protocol. Test-retest Spearman is not automatically a ceiling on predictor correlation; attenuation corrections require additional measurement-error assumptions. The completed even/odd split is a disjoint-pool check rather than two independent corpus draws. Chanin (2026) is the reason reviewers will ask for it. Cost: one more run per setting, about 40 minutes total on the GB10.
2. **Alpha sweep and matched-scale steering.** Repeat GPT-2-small and Pythia at alpha in {0.5, 1, 2, 4} and at alpha equal to each feature's 95th-percentile activation conditional on firing. AxBench shows the factor drives everything; Mayne et al. motivate sensitivity to distribution and scale when interpreting steering vectors. Cost: minutes for the small models, an hour for Gemma and Llama if wanted.
3. **Residual-space collateral.** Save the downstream residual change as well as the SAE-feature change and report its reconstruction-error change and an explained-change energy score. The reconstruction norm ratio alone is not an explained fraction, and the score need not be nonnegative. Cui et al. show the residual is where suppressed behaviour hides. Cost: small code change, one rerun.
4. **Random-direction control.** Steer with 300 random unit directions, compute their "crowding" against the same decoder, and match contexts and decoder norms, and compare with the same effect-only adjustment in both arms. Similar or different within-arm correlations are descriptive; they do not by themselves identify a causal mechanism or prove a between-arm difference. Cost: one run per setting.
5. **KL as a third target and multiple-comparison correction.** kl_mean and kl_per_effect are already in every per_feature.csv, so the whole analysis runs on them at zero cost, and Holm or Benjamini-Hochberg over each tested predictor family supports multiplicity-adjusted association claims; a magnitude ranking does not test whether the winner exceeds the runner-up. Cost: an hour of code.
6. **Dense-panel exclusion.** Recompute collateral excluding downstream features with firing frequency above 0.1 (Sun et al.). Requires keeping per-panel-feature deltas; cheap for the small models.
7. **Dictionary variants.** Gemma Scope ships 65k widths and several L0s for the same layers, Llama Scope ships 32x; the paper's Appendix G did this for Llama only. Cost: 20 minutes per variant.
8. **Richer stability labels.** FEGA's PC1 variance-explained of the logit-change cloud, and Tan et al.'s fraction of anti-aligned contexts, as additional targets. Cost: small code change, one rerun.
9. **Causal test of crowding.** Train or download a lower-crowding dictionary (OrtSAE, C2R) for GPT-2-small or Pythia and compare collateral per unit effect against the vanilla dictionary at matched reconstruction. This is the experiment that would move crowding from correlate to cause. Cost: a day, and it is a separate paper-sized result.

## 5. Where this leaves our claims

No checked source directly refutes the particular four-setting estimates. The 7 September update adds close forecasting and geometry-control precedents, so broad novelty and "nobody has tested causality" claims should be removed. The literature also sharpens three things we should say plainly in the revision: the collateral count is blind to the reconstruction residual (Cui et al.); the fixed alpha is a protocol choice requiring sensitivity checks (AxBench; Mayne et al. motivate scale-related caution); and the predictor family that wins outside GPT-2 (direct-logit statistics in Pythia and Llama) is the same family Wang et al. found predicts steering utility, which is independent support for the setting-dependence story rather than a weakness of it.
