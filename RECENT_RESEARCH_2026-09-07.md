# Recent research relevant to SAE steering collateral

Checked 7 September 2026. This update includes directly relevant papers submitted through 4 September. Discovery used arXiv's newest-first sparse-autoencoder index and web searches for SAE evaluation and activation steering. Candidate dates, authors, and results below were checked on the linked arXiv pages; section and table references identify the numerical evidence. Search-engine results alone missed several August and September papers. This is a targeted literature update, not proof of exhaustive coverage.

The earlier assessment missed substantial overlap. Pre-intervention side-effect forecasting and geometry-guided reductions in behavioral collateral already exist. The defensible contribution here is the particular four-setting, per-feature collateral residualization, its baseline comparisons, and its robustness checks. I did not locate an exact duplicate of that analysis.

## Papers that most directly change the revision

**Ong, Sim, Zhang and Sun, “Forecasting Side Effects of Activation Steering,” arXiv:2608.11227v1.** The source lists submission on **28 July 2026**, despite the August identifier. They forecast a cross-effect matrix over **67 behaviors** in Gemma-3-4B, Gemma-3-12B and Qwen2.5-7B. Their propagation map is learned from unsteered representations and read through behavioral probes. Table 2's new-source Spearman values are **0.354, 0.393, 0.352**, versus **0.145, 0.135, 0.282** for raw cosine; split-half measurement reliability is **0.827, 0.890, 0.907**. These are correlations of behavioral profiles, not correlations across individual SAE features. They do better on generated behavior, held-out source/target evaluation, and measurement calibration. Cite this as direct related work and avoid claiming that forecasting side effects before intervention is itself new. Our distinguishing analysis uses cheap feature statistics, an SAE collateral count and explicit nuisance controls across the four specified dictionaries. [Source: Sections 3 and 5, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11227v1).

**Upadhyaya and Sikdar, “When Safety Speaks a Language,” arXiv:2608.29936v1, 30 August.** This is the closest new geometry-to-collateral comparison: safety-feature interventions change output language and fluency, with costs tracking decoder alignment to language features across **three models and eight languages**. Appendix F selects Qwen features with low language-direction cosine: **0.029/0.038**, against **0.451/0.466** for the entangled selection. Language shift is approximately **0.01**, versus **-0.613** with the entangled features, with a smaller safety improvement. This is a targeted feature-selection intervention, not a matched vanilla-versus-OrtSAE training experiment. It nevertheless rules out a broad claim that no one has tested whether reducing geometric entanglement reduces collateral. Cite the behavioral validation and orthogonal-feature control explicitly; reserve the proposed training comparison for a narrower future-work claim. [Source: Section 4.3 and Appendix F](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.29936v1).

**Liu, Wang and Zou, “Enhancing SAE-based Steering via Neighbor Integrated Feature Selection,” arXiv:2608.28806v1, 28 August.** NIFS augments statistically selected features with representational neighbors and similarity-based weights. Feature splitting can give similar steering influence to features with very different statistical rankings. Table 1's Llama2-7B Diffmean sentiment success rises from **59.67 to 62.69**, and detoxification from **80.80 to 85.30**, with n-gram fluency also reported. They assess steering combinations and generated outputs, whereas our primary unit is a single feature. The implication is that decoder neighbors are not automatically harmful: their utility depends on whether their effects support the intended behavior. Describe our count as activation spread, not a count of semantically unrelated harms. An extension should compare intended effect and collateral jointly when adding neighbors. [Source: Sections 4–6, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.28806v1).

**Noël, “Where You Measure Decides What You Measure: Position Selection in Ablation-Based SAE Evaluation,” arXiv:2608.13337v1, 13 August.** The paper compares dictionary-dependent peak-activation positions with a common measurement position. In its controlled setup, dictionary-attributed variance of **7.6% and 11.9%** falls near zero after fixing the token. It also distinguishes raw and intervention-normalized readouts. Our final-token protocol specifies position, but selecting top/random/low contexts still depends on the dictionary, and different tokenizers need not identify identical textual endpoints. A future dictionary-width or training comparison must hold the actual contexts and token positions fixed. The paired random control now shares those indices with its SAE counterpart. This paper studies ablation and dictionary comparisons; it does not establish that our fixed-final-token additive results are invalid. [Source: abstract and Sections 4–6](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.13337v1).

**Bolik, Stöpler and Andrzejak, “Beyond a Bag of Features: Set-Level Instability in Sparse Autoencoders,” arXiv:2608.11197v1, 11 August.** Section 5.1 reports **20–60%** lost-latent rates when compatible adjectives are added to noun prompts. The measurement tracks the noun positions, and later analysis separates threshold effects, upstream detectability and possible interference. Their results caution against equating changed SAE support with changed human-interpretable concepts. Our thresholded activation-delta count is a different metric, so the numerical range does not transfer. The concrete revision is to distinguish activation spread from semantic collateral and report the residual and next-token changes as separate measurements. [Source: Sections 5.1–5.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11197v1).

## Newest causal and cross-model evidence

**Nguyen, Nguyen, Buntine and Le, “Recurrence Is Not Enough,” arXiv:2609.04808v1, 4 September; arXiv comments report BlackboxNLP 2026 acceptance.** **22 features per model** recur across four discovery settings, but only one per model transfers broadly in the causal tests. The full text qualifies the abstract: Gemma 3 amplification is significantly beneficial in **18 of 23** settings, not uniformly in every setting. Section 4.3 reports gains usually peaking around activation multipliers **2–3**, then saturating or reversing; these multipliers are not our additive alpha. They also test non-translation tasks. This is the newest directly relevant paper verified in this sweep. It supports a dose-response check and makes clear that activation recurrence, label reproducibility, and behavioral generalization are distinct questions. [Source: Sections 4.1–4.4 and conclusion](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04808v1).

**Ognev et al., “SharedSAE: One Feature Dictionary Across Language Models,” arXiv:2609.04344v1, 3 September.** SharedSAE retains **96.6%** of dedicated dictionaries' mean explained variance with **1.8 times** the post-hoc alignment correlation. Appendix N is more relevant than the abstract alone: among **280** candidate latents, median overlap of the top **30** causally promoted token strings is **0.19**, versus **0.27** for the linear logit-lens comparison and **0.00** for mismatched latents. The authors explicitly decline to equate shared labels with identical causal effects. Their coefficient sweep also uses matched-norm random-latent controls. This supplies a useful future route to comparing a common latent across models, while reinforcing that our four unrelated dictionaries do not isolate architecture as a causal factor. [Source: abstract and Appendix N](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.04344v1).

**Ding et al., “REINS: Refusal-Enhanced Inhibitory Steering with Sparse Autoencoder Features,” arXiv:2608.28233v1, 28 August; arXiv comments report EMNLP 2026 main acceptance.** REINS combines suppression of harmful-continuation features with amplification of refusal features. It distinguishes useful refusals from apparent safety caused by generation collapse and evaluates general capabilities. Its GUISE benchmark contains **900** wrapped harmful prompts over **45** scenarios, with matched safe counterparts. This is useful behavioral evaluation precedent, not another feature-statistic-versus-collateral regression. The revision should acknowledge that our KL target cannot separate intended steering, capability loss, and incoherent output. [Source: abstract, Section 5 and Appendix A.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.28233v1).

## Earlier papers whose relevance remains

**FEGA, Hoang et al., arXiv:2607.24645v1, 27 July.** It studies the cloud of logit changes from removing active features across contexts. Our centered PC1 ratio is inspired by that geometry, but additive steering with a mixed context sample is not a replication of feature removal. Report it as a supplementary summary. [Source: abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24645).

**CircuitSteer, Saadatinia et al., arXiv:2608.05732v1, 6 August.** It combines co-activation and decoder geometry to select interventions across layers and evaluates behavioral effectiveness and fluency. Its fluency claim is relative to its evaluated methods and tasks, not a universal ranking. It is an intervention-construction method, whereas our result assesses predictors under a fixed intervention protocol. [Source: abstract and method](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.05732v1).

**Gupte, Zhang and Khalili, arXiv:2608.25941v1, 26 August.** This studies SAE robustness under pruning, with activation geometry affecting preservation. It is adjacent evidence about moving the underlying model, rather than a direct steering-collateral precedent. Keep it secondary to the papers above. [Source: abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25941).

The earlier assessment's Chanin benchmark-reliability paper, Cui residual-recovery paper, Korznikov random-baseline paper, SAE-TS and AxBench remain relevant. The current code audit adds two mathematical qualifications: test-retest Spearman is not automatically a predictor ceiling, and a reconstruction norm ratio is not an explained fraction. Mayne et al. study encoding steering vectors through an SAE; applying that finding to our fixed additive coefficient is motivation for a sensitivity check, not evidence that alpha = 1 is out of distribution in every setting.

## What should change in the revision

1. Cite Ong et al. and Upadhyaya–Sikdar alongside SAE-TS as close precedents. Describe the exact controlled feature-level analysis as the contribution.
2. Keep the four-setting result, but say it is conditional on the model, dictionary, layer, coefficient, precision and measurement protocol. None of the checked papers directly tests and refutes those particular estimates.
3. Treat context-pool correlations as reproducibility, PC1 as a centered additive-effect summary, and KL as distribution change. None is by itself a behavioral-utility score.
4. Distinguish the residual reconstruction score from the norm ratio. A negative reconstruction score is possible because reconstructed and unrepresented changes need not be orthogonal.
5. Do not infer a causal crowding effect from partial correlations or a “significant versus nonsignificant” comparison with random directions. The matched vanilla-versus-regularized-dictionary experiment remains future work, alongside existing low-entanglement feature-selection controls.

Those were screened for scope rather than treated as duplicates of this language-model collateral analysis.
